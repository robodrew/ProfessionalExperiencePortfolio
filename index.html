1.1 Physical, social and intellectual development and characteristics of students

“Demonstrate knowledge and understanding of physical, social and intellectual development and characteristics of students and how these may affect learning.”

Evidence


Adapted IMS Learning plan and reflection on student characteristics

Commentary

This evidence demonstrates my understanding of students’ physical, social and intellectual development and how these characteristics influence learning. This is part of an IMS Learning module on ecology, originally designed for Grade 1 students. I, along with another pre-service teacher, was tasked with teaching this sequence to Foundation students early in the first term of the year. We adapted the lessons for these students to ensure the learning experiences were developmentally appropriate and accessible. I think this evidence shows that I meet this standard by observing students, using knowledge of their developmental characteristics to inform planning, and adjusting teaching strategies and learning activities to support engagement, participation and learner success.

Assignment excerpt exploring the impact of First Nations cultural and linguistic background on schooling experience

Commentary

This excerpt demonstrates my understanding of how culture, cultural identity and linguistic background can influence the educational experiences of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander students. In my discussion of the hidden curriculum, I explored how unspoken expectations and norms within schools can pressure First Nations students to conform to dominant cultural and linguistic practices, potentially creating tension between students’ identities and school expectations. 1.6 Strategies to support full participation of students with disability

“Demonstrate broad knowledge and understanding of legislative requirements and teaching strategies that support participation and learning of students with disability.”

Evidence


A photo of a visual organiser displaying activities for the day ahead

Commentary

The various Commonwealth Discrimination Acts, the Victorian Equal Opportunity Act (2010), our States’ Charter of Human Rights and Responsibilities, and our Code of Conduct require us to ensure all students have an opportunity to learn, and that we make reasonable adjustments to account for the different needs, abilities and characteristics of learners. Often in considering the supports we can provide particular learners, we might discover changes, adjustments, or supports that can benefit all learners.

The Visual Organiser demonstrates how these principles can be translated into practical classroom strategies. Visual schedules, first-then structures, noise level indicators and other organisers can reduce barriers for students with disability, including students who are neurodivergent, experience anxiety or have emerging literacy skills. Importantly, these adjustments can benefit all learners by reinforcing routines, supporting independence and making expectations more accessible. I believe this shows I demonstrate this standard by identifying barriers to participation, implementing reasonable adjustments and recognising inclusive practices as opportunities to create learning environments in which all students can participate and succeed.

Discussion

I chose these pictures because I think they illustrate how ICT can expand learning opportunities for students.

Technologies such as digital whiteboards provide an opportunity for learners to collaborate and construct class understanding of different topics and ideas. For example, the whiteboard above involved learners examining their own and others’ language learning identities and understandings of the idea of culture.

4.5 Use ICT safely, responsibly and ethically

“Demonstrate an understanding of the relevant issues and the strategies available to support the safe, responsible and ethical use of ICT in learning and teaching.”

Evidence


A video resource about navigating content and contact risks online


A supportive response framework around managing screen time

Discussion

In addition to the beneficial potential of digital technologies and online spaces, educators must have an understanding of the associated risks. eSmart provides a typology of risk known as the “4C”s, comprising “Content (what they see), Contact (who they interact with), Conduct (how they behave), and Compulsion (how they manage screen use)”.

One strategy is to relate online risks to situations students are more familiar with, such as family outings, or playground interactions. The screenshot above is from a resource I showed to the class about content and contact risks. Through song, it likens digital content to exploring a city, encouraging students to acknowledge that “not everything is for me” and to seek the support of trusted adults for help navigating the space.

This video was embedded in another live video resource that alluded to a Moose character’s overuse of their device, which was discussed with the class. For managing screen usage, a supportive response framework such as the “Notice - Name - Normalise - Plan” framework (also from eSmart) can encourage learners to reflect on and device usage without feeling shamed. I modelled this with reference to the Moose character.
